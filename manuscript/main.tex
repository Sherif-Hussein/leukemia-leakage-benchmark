\begin{abstract}
Deep-learning models for leukemia detection in blood smears often report 97--100\% accuracy, yet few are used in practice. We measured how much survives on new patients: 106 patients with acute myeloid leukemia (34 with acute promyelocytic leukemia, APL) split by diagnosis date, C-NMC 2019 (73 subjects, lymphoblastic leukemia vs.\ normal), and a pre-registered independent cohort from another laboratory (24 APL, 105 other AML). The same models reached up to 95\% accuracy under image-level cross-validation but 69--90\% on new patients. Patient leakage inflated the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) by 0.04--0.11 when cells carried the patient's diagnosis; dataset shift added 0.17--0.24 on the later APL cohort. Leaky training misplaced the threshold rather than the ranking: tuned for 95\% recall, it recovered 32--66\% of APL cells. Scan time was confounded with APL in the development cohort. A pre-specified pipeline (conditional stain normalization, fine-tuned network, engineered features, ensemble) trained on 82 patients ranked APL with an AUROC of 0.81 in the later cohort and 0.86 (95\% CI 0.77--0.94) in the independent laboratory, and leukemic C-NMC subjects with 0.96; a hematology foundation model combined with engineered features reached 0.88 in the independent laboratory. Ranking transferred but the development threshold did not: every APL patient was detected, at a specificity of 0--0.17, and a new laboratory needs about 19 confirmed local APL cases to restore 95\% recall. Such models can prioritize smears for review, not rule out APL.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{cute} leukemia is usually first suspected on a peripheral blood smear \cite{khoury2022who}. Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) shows how much rides on that first look: patients can bleed to death within days unless all-trans retinoic acid is started as soon as APL is suspected, often before genetic confirmation arrives. Reading smears takes experience that is not available everywhere, which is why automated smear analysis has attracted so much work.

Many deep-learning models report 97--100\% accuracy for leukemia detection from single-cell images \cite{jammal2025causalformer,jammal2025neurobridge}, and weakly supervised models reach AUCs above 0.95 for APL \cite{manescu2023apl,eckardt2022apl}. Yet few of these systems are used in practice. Part of the gap may lie in how they are evaluated. When cells from the same patient appear in both training and test sets, a model can succeed by recognizing the patient---the stain, the smear, the day it was scanned---rather than the disease; when the label is the patient's diagnosis copied onto every cell, this shortcut is always available \cite{geirhos2020shortcut,degrave2021shortcuts}. And accuracy alone hides the error that matters most clinically: a sick patient called healthy. Similar problems have been documented across machine-learning-based science \cite{kapoor2023leakage} and medical imaging \cite{zech2018confounding,roberts2021pitfalls,varoquaux2022failures}.

We measure these effects on a public cohort of 106 patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML), 34 with APL, repeat the analysis on the C-NMC 2019 dataset of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), and test the final pipelines, pre-registered, on an independent cohort from another laboratory. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\Ccontrib{1}] \emph{Leakage separated from shift.} With identical data and models, we compare image-level cross-validation (CV), patient-grouped CV and an external set, and split the gap into a leakage part and a shift part, for labels that belong to the patient and labels that belong to the cell; we also identify a scan-time confounder.
\item[\Ccontrib{2}] \emph{Three kinds of external test.} A temporal split of the APL-AML cohort, a held-out subject fold of C-NMC, and a pre-registered test on 129 patients from an independent laboratory, complemented by pooled CV over all 106 APL-AML patients.
\item[\Ccontrib{3}] \emph{Sensitivity-first reporting.} Thresholds are fixed on development data for a recall of at least 0.95; we report missed patients, specificity, F1 score, negative predictive value (NPV), calibration and net benefit, and how many local cases a new laboratory needs to restore the operating point.
\item[\Ccontrib{4}] \emph{Design decisions driven by the data.} An exploratory analysis and a label-free shift test decide the preprocessing; engineered, ImageNet, fine-tuned and hematology-foundation-model features, principal component analysis (PCA) and early stopping are each reported before and after.
\item[\Ccontrib{5}] \emph{Pre-specified pipelines with their cost.} A primary and a low-complexity secondary pipeline, both trainable on a CPU, are reported with storage, model size and time per patient.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Table~\ref{tab:survey} summarizes representative studies and the open question each leaves, linked to the contribution of this paper that addresses it.

\emph{Cell-level classification.} The C-NMC 2019 challenge \cite{gupta2022cnmc} held out whole subjects for testing; ResNeXt models reached a weighted F1 score of 0.889 \cite{prellberg2019cnmc} and a five-CNN ensemble 0.886 \cite{mondal2021ensemble}. Transformer and hybrid designs report 0.97--1.00 accuracy on C-NMC and ALL-IDB \cite{labati2011allidb,jammal2025causalformer,jammal2025neurobridge}, and CNN features with classical classifiers reach 0.88 on three classes \cite{kasim2025multiclass}. Because the split unit is often not reported, and some studies combine sources in which class and source coincide, it remains open how much of this carries over to new patients. A subject-disjoint benchmark on C-NMC found an AUROC of 0.913 \cite{albzour2026leakage}, for a single cell-level label.

\emph{Patient-level diagnosis.} Expert-level single-cell classification \cite{matek2019blast,matek2021bmc} enabled multiple instance learning (MIL) for genetic AML subtypes, including APL, in a cohort from the Munich Leukemia Laboratory \cite{hehr2023scemila} that we use here as an independent test set and mutation status \cite{wei2025annotationfree,eckardt2022npm1}. Sidhom \emph{et al.} \cite{sidhom2021apl} released the APL-AML cohort used here, validated their model on the later patients and compared it with ten clinicians; Manescu \emph{et al.} \cite{manescu2023apl} reported an AUC of 0.96 on the same patients under random three-fold CV, and Eckardt \emph{et al.} detected APL in bone-marrow smears \cite{eckardt2022apl}. Engineered morphometry has held up in an external cohort for myelodysplasia \cite{dealmeida2023haemorasis}. The number of missed patients at a pre-set operating point, and calibration, are rarely reported.

\emph{Foundation models and shift.} Hematology foundation models such as DinoBloom \cite{koch2024dinobloom}, built on DINOv2 \cite{oquab2024dinov2}, and their successors \cite{muminov2025cytodino,zamora2026retrieval} aim at robustness to scanners and stains; LeukemiaAttri images the same films with different microscopes \cite{rehman2024leukemiaattri}, and a DinoBloom-based transformer trained on 6,610 individuals was validated externally, including on our cohort \cite{dasdelen2026caitomorph}. In histopathology, stain normalization and augmentation trade off differently across shifts \cite{tellez2019stain}.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Datasets}
\emph{APL-AML.} The public cohort of Sidhom \emph{et al.} \cite{sidhom2021apl} contains single-cell images from Wright-stained peripheral blood smears of 106 adults with AML (34 APL, 72 other subtypes) treated at Johns Hopkins Hospital between 2010 and 2020 and captured with a CellaVision DM100 analyzer. The authors split it by date: 82 patients diagnosed before January 2019 form the Discovery cohort and 24 diagnosed later the Validation cohort. Of 25,724 images (about $360\times363$ pixels), 15,632 carry an operator-confirmed analyzer cell type (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}; examples in Fig.~\ref{fig:samples}; composition in Fig.~\ref{S-fig:dataset}). Ten files (0.04\%) are truncated and unreadable; they are kept as missing rows and imputed inside each training fold, and 54 are full microscope fields rather than single-cell crops. APL makes up 27\% of Discovery and 50\% of Validation patients, and the number of cells per patient is confounded with the diagnosis in opposite directions in the two cohorts. The original authors note that all Discovery smears scanned before 2018 were APL \cite{sidhom2021apl}; we examine this in Section~\ref{sec:date}.

\emph{C-NMC 2019.} To test whether the findings hold in another laboratory, we used C-NMC 2019 \cite{gupta2022cnmc}, released by The Cancer Imaging Archive \cite{mourya2019cnmcdata} and obtained from a Kaggle mirror whose file and subject counts match the official training set. It contains 10,661 segmented cells ($450\times450$ pixels, black background) from 73 subjects: 47 with B-lineage ALL and 26 healthy individuals whose normal B cells serve as controls (Table~\ref{S-tab:dataset_cnmc}; Fig.~\ref{fig:samples}). Before any analysis we designated official folds 0--1 as development set (42 subjects, 7,108 cells) and fold 2 as external set (31 subjects, 17 with ALL, 3,553 cells). Every cell carries its subject's diagnosis; unlike APL-AML, the ALL cells were selected as malignant lymphoblasts, so the label is also partly a property of the cell.

\emph{Independent APL cohort (MLL).} To test the final pipelines on patients from another institution, we used the AML-Cytomorphology\_MLL\_Helmholtz collection of Hehr \emph{et al.} \cite{hehr2023scemila} (The Cancer Imaging Archive, CC BY 4.0; obtained from a Kaggle mirror with one folder per patient). It contains single-cell images ($144\times144$ pixels, Metafer scanner, $40\times$) from peripheral blood smears of 189 patients of the Munich Leukemia Laboratory diagnosed 2009--2020. We used the 24 patients with APL (PML::RARA) as positives and the 105 patients with AML with NPM1 mutation, CBFB::MYH11 or RUNX1::RUNX1T1 as negatives, with up to 200 randomly drawn cells each (25,800 cells); the 60 healthy donors were excluded. The cohort was never used for training, selection, thresholds or calibration, and its analysis plan was committed to the code repository before any model was scored on it (commit \texttt{acf173b}).

\subsection{Preprocessing, Segmentation and Stain Normalization}
Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc} outlines the pipeline. A manifest records every image with its patient, cohort, diagnosis and cell label; no image is removed because of its label. For the engineered features, each image is resized to $192\times192$ pixels and separated into hematoxylin, eosin and residual channels by color deconvolution \cite{ruifrok2001deconvolution}. The nucleus is segmented by Otsu thresholding \cite{otsu1979} of the smoothed hematoxylin channel, followed by morphological opening and selection of the component nearest the image center. Pale cytoplasm could not be separated reliably from neighboring red cells, so we describe it with two proxies: a basophilic region of blue-dominant pixels within one nuclear radius and a perinuclear ring 2--9 pixels wide (Fig.~\ref{S-fig:seg}). For C-NMC, whose black background would be read as dense chromatin, pixels whose brightest channel is below 15 were set to white before engineered features were extracted; the CNN received the original images.

\label{fig:preproc}

For the CNN we compared no preprocessing, edge sharpening (unsharp mask), contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE), Reinhard color normalization \cite{reinhard2001color} and Macenko stain normalization \cite{macenko2009stain}, with a rule fixed in advance: the method had to reduce the measurable cohort shift without lowering external performance. Macenko normalization estimates the two stain directions of each image in optical-density space and maps them, and their concentrations, onto reference stains estimated from 300 Discovery images. Because smear backgrounds are beige rather than white, the illumination of each image is taken from the 99th percentile of each color channel. Normalization is applied only when a label-free shift test is positive: a classifier assigns control-class cells (AML cells, normal B cells) to the development or external set with patient-grouped 5-fold CV, and a permutation test that shuffles the set labels between patients gives the $p$-value. The test reads the unlabeled external images but none of their diagnoses; it is transductive in the sense that a new site would run it on its own unlabeled images before deployment. Engineered features were always computed on the original images, because their color features are diagnostic in APL.

\subsection{Feature Representations}
The 93 engineered features describe nuclear shape (9), the basophilic region and nucleus-to-region ratio (10), stain and color in the nucleus and the ring (26), chromatin texture from gray-level co-occurrence matrices \cite{haralick1973texture} and nuclear entropy (16), uniform local binary patterns \cite{ojala2002lbp} (28), and pale nuclear spots and dark granules (4). As a deep representation we used a frozen EfficientNet-B0 \cite{tan2019efficientnet} pretrained on ImageNet (1,280 dimensions per cell); the fusion representation concatenates both after column-wise standardization. Because a frozen generic encoder may understate deep learning, we also fine-tuned EfficientNet-B0. Full fine-tuning was not feasible on our CPU-only workstation, so we froze the stem and first five stages, cached their output, and trained the last two stages, the head and a new classifier with AdamW \cite{loshchilov2019adamw} (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, cosine schedule, 6 epochs, best epoch on 15\% of the training patients, up to 5,000 cells per fold, one seed; Table~\ref{S-tab:hparams}). As a hematology foundation model we used DinoBloom-S \cite{koch2024dinobloom}, a ViT-S/14 trained with DINOv2 \cite{oquab2024dinov2} on more than 380,000 blood and bone-marrow cells; its weights were taken unchanged from the authors' release, and the 384-dimensional class token of each $224\times224$ cell image (original colors) was used as a frozen representation, alone or concatenated with the engineered features.

On the frozen embeddings we tested PCA, fitted on the training cells of each fold. The embeddings are standardized and projected onto the leading $k$ eigenvectors $\mathbf{W}_k$ of their covariance \cite{jolliffe2016pca}, $\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}_k^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$. We pre-specified $k\in\{16,32,64\}$ and added $k\in\{4,8,128,256\}$ as a sensitivity analysis. A patient vector holds five statistics per dimension, so PCA reduces it from 6,400 to $5k$ values. The pooled component scores are centered and divided by one common scale (``natural scale''), so that the components keep their relative variances; standardizing each component separately would whiten them and give noise directions the same weight as the leading ones. Our first analysis used whitening; every PCA result in this paper uses the natural scale unless labeled otherwise. Further feature-engineering steps (correlation filter, per-slide stain normalization of features, L1 selection, a predicted differential count) are described in Table~\ref{S-tab:hparams} and reported in Table~\ref{S-tab:fe}.

\subsection{Classification Models}
Fig.~\ref{S-fig:model} shows how the components fit together. At cell level we used L2-regularized logistic regression (LR) and LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm} with class-balanced weights, each as a single pipeline that imputes and scales with training data only. For patients and subjects (T3, T5), up to 500 randomly sampled cells represent each patient. The pooled model summarizes every feature by its mean, standard deviation and 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles and applies LR. Gated attention MIL \cite{ilse2018abmil} maps each cell to $\mathbf{h}_k=\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_k)$ (128 units), weights the cells by
\begin{equation}
a_k=\frac{\exp\{\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\tanh(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{h}_k)\odot\sigma(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_k))\}}{\sum_{j}\exp\{\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\tanh(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{h}_j)\odot\sigma(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_j))\}},
\end{equation}
and classifies $\mathbf{z}=\sum_k a_k\mathbf{h}_k$; because the weights sum to one, the output does not depend on the number of cells. It is trained for 40 epochs with AdamW (learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$), class-weighted cross-entropy and random removal of 20\% of cells. The fine-tuned CNN gives a patient score as the mean of its cell probabilities.

\subsection{Final Pipelines and Pre-Specification}
\label{sec:final}
Before the final analysis we fixed one primary and one secondary pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipelines}) and committed the specification to the code repository (commit \texttt{f47f3ec}, 21 October 2026, 07:19 UTC+3). The primary pipeline normalizes stains when the shift test is positive, scores every cell with the fine-tuned CNN, and averages the percentile scores of three patient-level members---the mean fine-tuned cell probability, engineered features with pooled LR, and engineered features with attention MIL---with no weights fitted. The secondary pipeline was declared as a low-complexity option: stain normalization, the frozen CNN, PCA with 64 components, pooled statistics and LR. Both use a threshold fixed on development out-of-fold scores for a recall of at least 0.95. Their intended use is to prioritize smears for urgent review and confirmatory testing, not to rule APL out.

\emph{What the pre-specification does and does not protect.} The APL-AML Validation cohort was never used to fit a model, threshold or calibration. It was, however, not a single-use test set. In our first analysis (20 October 2026) and during this revision we evaluated many configurations on it---feature-engineering steps, PCA sizes, preprocessing variants on a subset---and those numbers were known when later choices were made, including the pre-specification. The primary pipeline was assembled from the components that had been most consistent across both datasets, but we cannot exclude that knowledge of Validation results shaped it. The APL-AML Validation numbers should therefore be read as those of a reused test set; the C-NMC external fold (also evaluated for several configurations, but never used for selection), the pooled analysis and the decomposition into leakage and shift provide the more protected evidence, and the independent MLL cohort, analyzed once under a plan committed beforehand, provides an untouched test. For that test we also pre-declared one model added in this revision, DinoBloom-S combined with engineered features and pooled LR, chosen because it had the highest development CV on C-NMC before the independent cohort was scored.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Metrics}
Every cell-level model was evaluated in three ways (Fig.~\ref{S-fig:splits}): 5-fold CV on the development set with image-level folds, as is common in the literature; 5-fold CV with patient-grouped folds; and the external set. We define \emph{leakage} as image-level minus patient-grouped CV AUROC (same data, only the split differs) and \emph{shift} as patient-grouped CV minus external AUROC (new patients). We also scored the external set with the models trained under image-level folds, to see whether leaky training harms the model or only the estimate. Within each training fold, an inner 80/20 split of the same kind fitted a calibration temperature \cite{guo2017calibration}. External predictions of patient-level models average all fold models over three seeds.

Since missing an APL patient can delay life-saving treatment, whereas a false alarm leads to a confirmatory genetic test, every decision threshold was fixed in advance on development out-of-fold scores to reach a recall of at least 0.95. At that operating point we report recall with the number of missed positives, specificity, F1 score, NPV and accuracy; AUROC as a threshold-free summary; and calibration as the Brier score, calibration intercept and slope \cite{vancalster2019calibration} and expected calibration error (ECE). Confidence intervals (95\%) come from bootstrap resampling of patients \cite{efron1986bootstrap}; differences between models use a paired patient-level bootstrap, with Holm correction \cite{holm1979} when several comparisons share a reference. C-NMC was analyzed with the same protocol.

Three further analyses address what the external tests alone cannot. First, because 24 external patients give wide intervals \cite{riley2021samplesize,collins2016samplesize}, we repeated the main patient-level comparisons with three repeats of 5-fold CV over all 106 APL-AML patients, stratified by diagnosis and cohort, with thresholds chosen by inner CV. Second, we simulated local recalibration: $k$ confirmed local patients ($k/2$ APL, $k=2,\dots,8$) are drawn from the Validation cohort, the threshold is lowered if needed to just below the lowest local APL score, and the remaining $24-k$ patients are scored, over 2,000 draws. Third, decision-curve analysis \cite{vickers2006dca} estimated the net benefit at an APL prevalence of 10\%, after logistic recalibration on Discovery and a shift to the target prevalence.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Exploratory Analysis and the Shift Test}
\label{sec:eda}
Both datasets are imbalanced in different ways: APL makes up 27\% of Discovery and 50\% of Validation patients; ALL makes up 71\% of C-NMC development and 55\% of external subjects. We therefore used class-balanced weights, did not resample cells, set thresholds for the positive class, and report recall, specificity, missed cases, F1 score and AUROC rather than accuracy alone.

Fig.~\ref{fig:eda} summarizes the APL-AML exploratory analysis. Every feature group shifted between cohorts, stain and color most (median standardized mean difference 0.34). Among AML patients, a classifier separated Validation from Discovery cells with an AUROC of 0.753 on stain and color features (permutation $p=0.005$), 0.704 on all engineered features ($p=0.010$) and 0.583 on frozen CNN embeddings ($p=0.039$; Table~\ref{S-tab:shift}). Thirty-seven feature pairs were nearly redundant ($|r|>0.9$), and single-feature patient-level AUROCs in the two cohorts correlated only moderately ($r=0.38$). C-NMC looked different (Fig.~\ref{S-fig:eda_cnmc}): feature shifts were small (median 0.04--0.16 per group) and normal B cells could not be assigned to their set (AUROC 0.44--0.47 on the full data, $p\geq0.45$ for every representation; 0.52--0.56 on the preprocessing subset, Table~\ref{tab:preproc}). By the pre-stated rule, stain normalization was therefore applied to APL-AML only. On the full data, however, CNN embeddings of normalized images were slightly \emph{more} identifiable than before (0.603 vs.\ 0.583, $p=0.06$), whereas on the subset used for the selection normalization had lowered identifiability from 0.575 to 0.543. Macenko normalization reduces color differences but does not remove the acquisition signatures the CNN can see, and its selection rested on a subset that included Validation patients; Section~\ref{sec:finalres} therefore reports the primary pipeline with and without it.

\subsection{Leakage Versus Shift}
Table~\ref{tab:leakage} and Fig.~\ref{S-fig:leakage} separate the two sources of optimism. When each cell carried its patient's diagnosis (T1), image-level CV reported AUROCs of 0.938--0.987, patient-grouped CV 0.867--0.901 and the external cohort 0.658--0.734. Leakage accounted for 0.04--0.11 of the gap and shift for 0.17--0.24; both components were positive in every patient-level bootstrap interval (leakage lower bounds 0.02--0.07, shift lower bounds 0.05--0.07), although the shift intervals are wide (upper bounds 0.31--0.43) because only 24 patients form the external cohort. When the label described the cell itself (T2), leakage was 0.01--0.03 (intervals excluding zero) and shift indistinguishable from zero. On C-NMC cells (T4), leakage (0.07--0.08, intervals excluding zero) was larger than shift (0.02--0.06, intervals including zero), consistent with a random subject-disjoint fold of one collection. Leakage is thus largest when the label belongs to the patient, as expected, but on the temporally split APL-AML cohort the shift between periods was the larger part of the overstatement.

Leaky training did not produce worse models. Classifiers trained under image-level folds ranked external cells as well as or slightly better than those trained under grouped folds (T1: 0.677--0.760 vs.\ 0.658--0.734), presumably because each fold model saw cells of every patient. What leakage corrupted was the operating point: a threshold tuned for a recall of 0.95 on leaky folds recovered only 32--66\% of the cells of new APL patients, against 69--81\% for thresholds tuned on grouped folds. Table~\ref{tab:reported} shows the result in the terms most studies report: the same models reached 0.88--0.95 accuracy under image-level CV at a 0.5 threshold, but 0.69--0.73 on new APL-AML patients, 0.87--0.90 for blasts and 0.82--0.83 on C-NMC.

\subsection{Scan Time as a Confounder}
\label{sec:date}
The images carry no scan dates, but the analyzer numbers the cells it signs sequentially, so the median cell number of a patient is a time proxy. It separated Validation from Discovery patients almost perfectly (AUROC 0.992), which supports its validity. Within Discovery, it predicted APL with an AUROC of 0.775 (Table~\ref{tab:dateconf}). In the Validation cohort it carried no diagnostic information (0.479), so the confounding is confined to the development data. The images themselves record scan time: among AML patients only, pooled features separated early from late scans with AUROCs of 0.74--0.79 for every representation, highest for all engineered features (0.794) and including shape and texture (0.767); Macenko normalization lowered this for color features from 0.760 to 0.658 but did not remove it. To see whether the diagnostic models depend on it, we repeated the Discovery CV in the 31 patients (18 APL) scanned while both diagnoses were being scanned, where scan time was no longer positively associated with APL (AUROC 0.325, i.e., within this window later scans were, if anything, more often APL). Engineered features kept their performance (CV AUROC 0.927 on all patients, 0.929 in the window), whereas the frozen CNN dropped from 0.957 to 0.858, stain and color features from 0.881 to 0.805, and engineered features computed on normalized images from 0.869 to 0.729. In every case the model score added information beyond scan time (likelihood-ratio $p<0.001$), but scores and scan time were correlated (Spearman 0.23--0.55). Part of the high development performance of CNN and color features therefore rests on time-linked acquisition differences, which would show up as ``shift'' when the model meets a later cohort; the 31-patient window is small, and these estimates are imprecise.

\subsection{Choosing the Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preproc}
Table~\ref{tab:preproc} compares preprocessing options on a fixed subset of up to 60 cells per patient. Edge sharpening raised development performance (cells 0.873 to 0.887) but lowered external performance (cells 0.661 to 0.631; patients 0.667 to 0.639), widening the gap. CLAHE changed little (external 0.667 for cells and patients) and did not reduce the cohort shift (0.575 to 0.581). Reinhard normalization removed most of the shift (0.524) but lowered external cell AUROC (0.644). Macenko normalization reduced the shift (0.543) and was the only option that raised external performance at both levels (cells 0.690, patients 0.694), so it met the rule. Estimating stain vectors once per patient was faster but lowered patient-level development CV by 0.017 and failed the pre-specified rule, so normalization is done per cell (0.071\,s per cell). On C-NMC, where no shift was detected, Macenko normalization lowered external AUROC slightly (subjects 0.882 to 0.857), supporting conditional use. These differences are small compared with the uncertainty of 24 external patients, and the subset included Validation patients, as disclosed above.

\subsection{Engineered, Frozen and Fine-Tuned Features}
For blast detection on new patients, engineered features with LightGBM reached an AUROC of 0.957 (95\% CI 0.931--0.977) against 0.920 for frozen EfficientNet-B0 embeddings (difference 0.037; paired bootstrap 95\% CI 0.008--0.065), and on C-NMC cells 0.840 against 0.775 (Table~\ref{tab:finetune}). Fine-tuning closed this gap: external AUROC rose to 0.773 for T1 (frozen 0.694), 0.960 for blasts and 0.841 on C-NMC cells. For blasts it also gave a better operating point than the engineered features with LightGBM (specificity 0.83 instead of 0.62 at a recall of 0.95--0.99, F1 0.74 instead of 0.58) and than the frozen CNN (specificity 0.64, F1 0.58). In an ablation, removing the stain and color group cost blast detection the most ($-0.034$ AUROC, 95\% CI $-0.060$ to $-0.015$; Table~\ref{S-tab:ablation}, Fig.~\ref{S-fig:impact}). For T1, the threshold chosen on Discovery flagged almost every Validation cell (recall 0.996, specificity 0.17), the operating-point problem seen throughout the temporal cohort.

\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction: Before and After PCA}
\label{sec:pca}
Table~\ref{tab:pcaimpact} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pca} show the effect of PCA on the pooled patient model (natural scale; full sweep in Table~\ref{S-tab:pca}). Without reduction, each patient is described by 6,400 values, about 100 per APL-AML training patient.

On APL-AML without stain normalization, PCA raised external AUROC from 0.708 to 0.792 with 64 components but lowered the decisions at the clinical threshold (6 instead of 8 of 12 APL patients detected; F1 0.57 instead of 0.67). On C-NMC it cost a little on every metric (AUROC 0.908 without, 0.853 with 64 components; 16 instead of 17 ALL subjects detected). In pooled CV over all 106 APL-AML patients, 32 and 64 components ranked as well as the unreduced embedding ($\Delta$AUROC $+0.000$ and $+0.006$; Table~\ref{S-tab:pooled}), and 16 components were worse ($-0.057$). The pre-declared rule (highest development CV among $k\in\{16,32,64\}$) selected 64 on both datasets.

PCA and stain normalization interacted. After Macenko normalization, PCA-64 no longer improved ranking: external AUROC fell from 0.701 to 0.639 and F1 from 0.79 to 0.76, with recall unchanged (11 of 12). Normalization had already removed part of the cohort-specific color variation that, without it, PCA removed together with some signal. PCA keeps directions of large variance, not those that separate the classes, and the external disease signal grew steadily with the number of components on both datasets (Fig.~\ref{fig:pca}c). We therefore use PCA only to reduce complexity (Section~\ref{sec:finalres}), not as a way to gain accuracy.

Whitening was a pitfall: with each component standardized, development CV rose to 0.99 while external AUROC on C-NMC fell from 0.849 (8 components) to 0.655 (256), the signature of a classifier fitting noise directions (dashed lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:pca}).

\subsection{Training Settings: Epochs and Early Stopping}
\label{sec:training}
We compared the fixed 40 epochs of attention MIL with a data-driven alternative: inside every training fold, nested 3-fold patient-level CV chose the epoch with the lowest validation loss. This did not help (Table~\ref{S-tab:epochs}). External AUROC changed by less than 0.01 on APL-AML and fell by 0.02--0.06 on C-NMC, and F1 and calibration did not improve. Because the 80/20 calibration split is taken first, each inner model was trained on about 35 and validated on about 17 APL-AML patients (C-NMC: 18 and 9), and the chosen epoch ranged from 1 to 40 for the same model, so early stopping made results less stable across seeds. We kept the fixed schedule. Learning curves (Fig.~\ref{S-fig:training}, computed with whitened PCA inputs from our first analysis and after the configuration was fixed) show that held-out AUROC plateaued after about 10 epochs while held-out loss rose, i.e., the fixed schedule cost no ranking but produced overconfident probabilities. Replacing average pooling of the fine-tuned CNN by attention pooling over spatial positions changed development AUROC by $-0.020$, $+0.014$ and $+0.005$ on three folds and was not pursued.

\subsection{Final Pipelines on New Patients}
\label{sec:finalres}
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} trace how each component changed performance on new patients (all six benchmark patient-level models and their confusion matrices are in Table~\ref{S-tab:patient} and Fig.~\ref{S-fig:confusion}); Table~\ref{tab:tradeoff} sets the pipelines against their cost.

\emph{Primary pipeline.} On APL-AML, the primary pipeline detected all 12 APL patients in the Validation cohort (recall 1.00, exact 95\% CI 0.74--1.00; AUROC 0.806, 0.615--0.956), but flagged 10 of 12 AML patients (specificity 0.17, 0.02--0.48; F1 0.71). On C-NMC (no normalization, because no shift was detected) it reached an AUROC of 0.960 and detected 16 of 17 ALL subjects (recall 0.94, 0.71--1.00) at a specificity of 0.79 (0.49--0.95; F1 0.89). With 12 APL patients, a perfect observed recall is compatible with a true recall as low as 0.74. The ensemble did not rank better than its best member (APL-AML: attention MIL 0.812; C-NMC: fine-tuned CNN 0.962), but it gave the most balanced operating point: on APL-AML it matched the pooled engineered model (no missed patient, specificity 0.17) where attention MIL flagged every AML patient and the fine-tuned CNN missed two APL patients; on C-NMC it kept the specificity of the pooled model (0.79) where the fine-tuned CNN reached 0.57. Its value is robustness of the operating point, not better ranking.

\emph{What each step contributed.} Fine-tuning was the most consistent improvement at cell level on both datasets. Stain normalization helped the frozen CNN at the operating point (1 instead of 4 missed APL patients; F1 0.79 instead of 0.67) but not the fine-tuned CNN (AUROC 0.771 vs.\ 0.778), which appears to absorb much of the color shift itself.

\emph{Sensitivity to stain normalization.} Because Macenko normalization was selected on a subset that included Validation patients and did not reduce CNN-detectable shift on the full data, we re-ran the primary pipeline without it. External AUROC fell from 0.806 to 0.785, with the same operating point (12 of 12 APL patients detected, specificity 0.17), so the reported result does not depend on normalization. Computing the engineered features on normalized images as well, instead of on the originals, lowered external AUROC to 0.663 (engineered pooled LR alone: 0.757 to 0.576) and missed one APL patient: normalization removes the color information that makes engineered features diagnostic, which supports applying it to the CNN branch only (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}).

\emph{Secondary pipeline and the cost of PCA.} With PCA-64, the patient-level classifier has 321 instead of 6,401 parameters and stores 256 instead of 5,120 bytes per cell; the classifier itself fits in half the time (0.13 vs.\ 0.26\,s), but fitting PCA adds about 6\,s per training set, so training as a whole is slower; attention MIL on the reduced inputs would have 7 times fewer parameters. The price on APL-AML was 0.06 AUROC (0.701 to 0.639), 0.03 F1 (0.79 to 0.76) and 0.08 specificity, with the same recall. Image-level steps dominate the time per patient---Macenko normalization 0.071\,s, the CNN 0.051\,s and engineered features 0.038\,s per cell---so the primary pipeline needs about 80\,s per 500-cell patient on our CPU, 45\% of it for normalization, and PCA mainly saves storage, memory and model size rather than time.

\subsection{A Hematology Foundation Model}
\label{sec:dino}
Table~\ref{tab:dino} compares frozen DinoBloom-S features with the other representations. On C-NMC cells, DinoBloom outperformed the frozen ImageNet network on development (0.859 vs.\ 0.834) and external data (0.801 vs.\ 0.775), and combined with engineered features it had the highest development CV of all cell-level models (0.882), which also gave the highest external AUROC (0.845) and F1 score (0.89). At subject level, the same combination with pooled LR again ranked best on development data (0.919) and detected all 17 leukemic subjects in the external fold (AUROC 0.962, specificity 0.71). On the later APL-AML cohort, DinoBloom with attention MIL had the highest development CV (0.924) and the highest external AUROC of any model in this study (0.868, 95\% CI 0.70--0.99), but at the development threshold it detected only 6 of 12 APL patients; combined with engineered features it missed one at a specificity of 0.58. Domain-specific pretraining thus improved ranking on all three test sets without fixing the operating point.

Before turning to DinoBloom we tried to improve the fine-tuned CNN on C-NMC, selecting on development CV only. Cropping each image to the cell raised cell-level development AUROC from 0.853 to 0.860 but lowered subject-level development AUROC (0.864 to 0.833), well within the variation between folds (0.64--0.94); fine-tuning one more network stage overfit within six epochs (training loss near zero, held-out AUROC 0.82--0.83 on the first two folds) and was stopped; and LightGBM on engineered features gained 0.012 in development CV but nothing on the external fold (0.839 vs.\ 0.840). A SHAP analysis of the engineered-feature classifier (Fig.~\ref{S-fig:shap}) attributed malignancy mostly to nuclear color, larger nuclear and cell size and local texture patterns, consistent with lymphoblast morphology, although the weight on color may partly reflect subject-specific staining.

\subsection{Independent External Test}
\label{sec:mll}
The independent cohort differed strongly from Discovery: AML cells of the two sources could be told apart almost perfectly (AUROC 0.999 on stain and color features, permutation $p\leq0.03$), so by the pre-specified rule Macenko normalization was applied to the CNN branch. Table~\ref{tab:mll} and Fig.~\ref{fig:mll} report the single pre-registered analysis. The primary pipeline, trained on 82 Discovery patients from another hospital, ranked the 129 independent patients with an AUROC of 0.862 (95\% CI 0.765--0.936), higher than on the later APL-AML cohort, and the pre-declared DinoBloom combination reached 0.877 (0.773--0.960). Engineered features carried most of the transfer (pooled LR 0.832), whereas the ImageNet networks transferred worst despite stain normalization (fine-tuned 0.737, secondary pipeline with PCA 0.660). The development threshold, however, did not transfer at all: the primary pipeline detected all 24 APL patients (recall 1.00, 95\% CI 0.86--1.00) but cleared none of the 105 AML patients, and the other models behaved similarly (specificity 0.00--0.18), except the secondary pipeline, which missed 6 APL patients.

Fig.~\ref{fig:mll}b shows what local cases would achieve. A threshold just below the lowest of $m$ confirmed local APL scores reached the expected recall $m/(m+1)$ for every model (0.84 with $m=5$, 0.92 with $m=12$), confirming the bound found on APL-AML with five times more patients. At equal recall, better ranking bought specificity: with $m=12$ the primary pipeline cleared 36\% of AML patients and the fine-tuned CNN 28\%.

\subsection{Calibration}
Patient-level probabilities were poorly calibrated on both external sets (Table~\ref{S-tab:calibration}, Fig.~\ref{S-fig:reliability}). On APL-AML, calibration intercepts were positive (1.0--1.3 for raw outputs), i.e., APL risk was underestimated, as expected when the APL prevalence rises from 27\% to 50\%; on C-NMC they were negative ($-0.7$ to $-1.6$) as the ALL prevalence fell from 71\% to 55\%. Slopes varied widely (0.3--5.4), and the Brier score of the primary pipeline after Platt scaling on development data was 0.231 on APL-AML and 0.136 on C-NMC. With 24 and 31 external patients, these estimates are imprecise, but they show that predicted probabilities should not be used as risks without local recalibration.

\subsection{Pooled Analysis and Local Recalibration}
\label{sec:recal}
In pooled CV over all 106 APL-AML patients (Table~\ref{S-tab:pooled}), the unreduced EfficientNet-B0 embedding ranked patients best (AUROC 0.953, with per-feature standardization; 0.929 at natural scale), better than engineered features with pooled statistics ($-0.059$, $p=0.007$ unadjusted, $p=0.052$ after Holm correction) and the promyelocyte fraction used as a manual biomarker (0.677). Thresholds chosen inside each training fold transferred to the held-out folds (recall 0.89--1.00), which supports the view that the failure on the Validation cohort reflects temporal shift; because patients from both periods were mixed, this analysis does not measure transfer to a later cohort.

Setting a new site's threshold just below the lowest score of $m=k/2$ local APL cases gave the same expected recall for every model, about $m/(m+1)$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:recal}a; Table~\ref{S-tab:recal}). This follows from exchangeability and does not depend on the model, so about 19 confirmed local APL cases are needed before such a threshold can be expected to reach a recall of 0.95. Lowering the development threshold only when a local APL case would otherwise be missed changed little for models whose development threshold already reached a recall of 0.92--1.00 on the Validation cohort; it mainly cost specificity (frozen CNN with Macenko: 0.58 to 0.42 with eight local cases), and raised the recall of the fine-tuned CNN only from 0.83 to 0.86 (Fig.~\ref{fig:recal}b). For the models with recall below 1.00, the probability of missing at least one APL patient among the remaining 16 stayed at 0.66--0.68. At an APL prevalence of 10\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:recal}c), the primary pipeline gave the highest net benefit at a 10\% threshold probability (0.050 against 0 for testing everyone and 0.017--0.042 for the other models), and benefits close to testing everyone below 5\%.

\section{Discussion}
Four findings stand out. First, how a model is evaluated changes the reported number more than how it is built, and the overstatement has two separable sources. Patient leakage inflated AUROC by up to 0.11 when cells carried their patient's diagnosis, but much less when the label described the cell; the shift to a later cohort added up to 0.24. Leaky training did not make the models worse rankers, but it made their thresholds wrong. A study that reports image-level CV therefore overstates both the ranking and, more seriously, the recall that can be expected in practice. This held on two datasets and may explain why subject-disjoint challenge entries report F1 scores near 0.89 rather than near 1.0 \cite{prellberg2019cnmc,mondal2021ensemble}.

Second, the temporal APL-AML split carries a confounder that a patient-grouped CV within the development cohort cannot remove: APL patients were scanned earlier than most AML patients. Restricting the Discovery cohort to the period in which both diagnoses were scanned left the diagnostic performance of engineered morphology features unaffected---although these features carry the most scan-time information of all (0.794 among AML patients)---but cost the frozen CNN 0.10 AUROC, which suggests that generic deep features partly learn when a smear was scanned, and that the large shift component on the temporal cohort is partly the unwinding of this confounding rather than a change in disease appearance. Studies on this cohort, ours included, should report how their results depend on scan time.

Third, the data decided which design choices helped. A label-free test for whether cells can be traced to their set found a stain shift in APL-AML and none in C-NMC, and this one observation explained several results: stain normalization helped modestly on APL-AML (the primary pipeline ranked 0.02 AUROC better with it, at the same operating point) and slightly hurt on C-NMC, development thresholds transferred to C-NMC but not to the later APL cohort, and PCA interacted with normalization. Fine-tuning and, more clearly, a hematology foundation model were the representation choices that helped on every test set; nested early stopping, attention pooling, cropping and gradient boosting did not improve on simpler settings, and PCA traded a little performance for a much smaller model. Across laboratories, interpretable engineered features transferred better than ImageNet networks, even after stain normalization, and combining them with DinoBloom gave the best ranking on the independent cohort.

Fourth, ranking transferred better than decisions, and this held on an untouched cohort from another laboratory. Trained on 82 patients from one hospital, the pre-specified pipeline ranked APL in 129 patients from another country with an AUROC of 0.86, higher than on the later cohort of its own hospital; but at the development threshold it flagged every patient there, as it flagged most AML patients in the later cohort. Every other model either missed patients or flagged most of the AML patients. A threshold set from $m$ local positives has an expected recall of only $m/(m+1)$, so about 19 confirmed local APL cases (about 38 local patients at equal numbers) are needed before a recall of 0.95 can be expected---a bound that the independent cohort confirmed for every model---and 24 external patients are far below the sample sizes recommended for external validation \cite{riley2021samplesize,collins2016samplesize}. For a condition in which a missed diagnosis can be fatal, a model like ours is best used to prioritize smears for urgent review and testing, with its threshold confirmed on local cases.

\emph{Limitations.} C-NMC differs in disease (ALL vs.\ normal B cells), so it tests the evaluation principles rather than the APL model; the independent MLL cohort tests the APL model itself, but with a different negative population (genetically defined AML subtypes) and without the healthy donors. The external sets are small, and the APL-AML Validation cohort was evaluated repeatedly during development and is best regarded as a reused test set (Section~\ref{sec:final}). The scan-time proxy is indirect. Cell labels in APL-AML come from an analyzer verified by an operator, and our cytoplasm features rely on proxies. Fine-tuning was partial, limited to 5,000 cells per fold and run with one seed on a CPU, and DinoBloom was used frozen and in its smallest version; stain augmentation \cite{tellez2019stain}, full fine-tuning and larger foundation models may perform better. No prospective or reader-in-the-loop evaluation was performed.

\section{Conclusion}
Reported accuracy for leukemia detection from blood smears depends strongly on how the evaluation is set up. Across two public datasets, splitting cells instead of patients inflated performance and, above all, the expected recall, while a temporal shift and a scan-time confounder accounted for the larger part of the gap on the APL cohort. A pipeline built step by step from the data---stain normalization only where a shift was detected, fine-tuned and interpretable features, and pre-specified combination and thresholds---missed no APL patient in a later cohort or in a pre-registered cohort from another laboratory, where it ranked patients with an AUROC of 0.86, and a hematology foundation model combined with engineered features ranked best (0.88). Thresholds did not transfer between sites, and restoring them requires about 19 confirmed local APL cases. We recommend that studies in this area split data by patient, separate leakage from shift, test for acquisition shift and confounding before choosing preprocessing, report recall, missed cases, specificity, F1 score, NPV and calibration at a pre-specified operating point, and report the computational cost of every component.
