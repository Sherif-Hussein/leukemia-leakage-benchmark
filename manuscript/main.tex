\begin{abstract}
Deep-learning models for leukemia detection in blood smears often report 97--100\% accuracy, yet few are used in practice. We examined how much of this performance survives honest evaluation on two public datasets: 106 patients with acute myeloid leukemia (34 with acute promyelocytic leukemia, APL; 25,724 cells) split by diagnosis date, and C-NMC 2019 (73 subjects; acute lymphoblastic leukemia vs.\ normal B cells; 10,661 cells) with a held-out official fold. With identical data and classifiers, splitting cells instead of patients inflated the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) by 0.25--0.32 and 0.09--0.14 on the two datasets when cells carried the patient's diagnosis, but by at most 0.04 when the label described the cell. Engineered morphology features matched or outperformed frozen ImageNet embeddings in most external comparisons (blast detection 0.957 vs.\ 0.920; C-NMC cells 0.840 vs.\ 0.775), and a fine-tuned CNN was best for single-cell APL classification (0.773) but not for patient diagnosis (0.778 vs.\ 0.812 for attention-based multiple instance learning). Principal component analysis (PCA) of CNN embeddings raised external AUROC on the APL test (0.708 to 0.792) but detected fewer APL patients at the clinical threshold, was neutral in pooled cross-validation over 106 patients, and slightly lowered all metrics on C-NMC (AUROC 0.908 to 0.840--0.870); standardizing each component (whitening) made results erratic. Thresholds fixed for 95\% sensitivity transferred to C-NMC (15--17 of 17 subjects detected) but not to the later APL cohort, where they missed up to half of APL patients; recalibrating with $m$ local APL cases yields an expected sensitivity of only $m/(m+1)$. We recommend patient-level splits, more than one external test, sensitivity-first reporting with missed-case counts, and local recalibration with about 20 confirmed positive cases before clinical use.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{cute} leukemia is usually first suspected on a peripheral blood smear, where a trained morphologist looks for blasts and other abnormal white cells \cite{khoury2022who}. Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) shows how much rides on that first look: it is a medical emergency, because patients can bleed to death within days unless all-trans retinoic acid is started as soon as APL is suspected, often before genetic confirmation arrives. Reading smears takes time and experience, and the experience is not available everywhere, which is why automated smear analysis has attracted so much work.

Published results look encouraging. Many deep-learning models report accuracies between 97\% and 100\% for leukemia detection from single-cell images \cite{jammal2025causalformer,jammal2025neurobridge}, and weakly supervised models reach AUCs above 0.95 for recognizing APL \cite{manescu2023apl}. Yet few of these systems are used in practice. Our starting point is that part of the gap lies not in the models but in how they are evaluated. When cells from the same patient appear in both the training and test sets, a model can succeed by recognizing the patient---the stain, the smear, the day it was scanned---rather than the disease. When the label is the patient's diagnosis copied onto every cell, this shortcut is always available. And when performance is summarized by accuracy alone, it hides the error that matters most clinically: a sick patient called healthy. Similar leakage and shortcut problems have been documented across machine-learning-based science \cite{kapoor2023leakage} and in medical imaging \cite{zech2018confounding,roberts2021pitfalls}.

In this work we measure these effects directly on a public cohort of 106 patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML), 34 of whom have APL, replicate the analysis on the C-NMC 2019 dataset of acute lymphoblastic leukemia, and build an evaluation that a clinician could trust. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\Ccontrib{1}] \emph{A controlled leakage audit on two datasets.} With identical data, features and classifiers, we compare image-level cross-validation (CV), patient-grouped CV and an untouched external set, both for a label that belongs to the patient and for a label that belongs to the cell.
\item[\Ccontrib{2}] \emph{External validation of two kinds.} Models are developed on an earlier APL-AML cohort and tested once on patients diagnosed later, and on C-NMC developed on two official folds and tested on the third; a pooled 106-patient analysis adds statistical power.
\item[\Ccontrib{3}] \emph{Sensitivity-first reporting.} Decision thresholds are set on the development cohort for a sensitivity of at least 0.95, and we report recall, the number of missed APL patients, specificity, F1 score, negative predictive value and calibration, not only accuracy and AUROC. We also quantify how many local cases a new laboratory needs to restore the operating point, and the net benefit at a realistic prevalence.
\item[\Ccontrib{4}] \emph{Interpretable, frozen and fine-tuned features.} We design 93 morphology features that mirror what hematologists look at, compare them with frozen and fine-tuned CNN features and their fusion, show through ablation which feature groups carry the signal, and test whether dimensionality reduction by principal component analysis (PCA) improves transfer, including a replication that it did not pass.
\item[\Ccontrib{5}] \emph{A lightweight patient-level pipeline.} Patient diagnosis is made by attention-based multiple instance learning (MIL) \cite{ilse2018abmil} over frozen features, trained on a CPU, and compared with established baselines on the same cohort.
\end{itemize}
Code, split files and label maps are released so that results on both datasets can be compared directly in future work.

\section{Related Work}
Table~\ref{tab:survey} summarizes representative studies. For each, the last column notes an open question that the study was not designed to answer and links it to the contribution of this paper that addresses it.

\subsection{Cell-Level Classification of Blood Smears}
Much of the literature classifies single segmented cells as leukemic or normal. The C-NMC 2019 challenge \cite{gupta2022cnmc} set a useful standard by holding out whole subjects for testing: ResNeXt models reached a weighted F1 score of 0.889 \cite{prellberg2019cnmc}, and a weighted ensemble of five CNNs reached 0.886 with an AUC of 0.941 \cite{mondal2021ensemble}. More recent transformer and hybrid designs report accuracies of 0.97 to 1.00 on C-NMC and on the ALL image dataset \cite{jammal2025causalformer,jammal2025neurobridge}, and hybrid pipelines that feed CNN features to classical classifiers reach 0.88 when separating lymphoblasts, myeloblasts and normal cells \cite{kasim2025multiclass}. These studies advanced architectures and training strategies. Because the split unit is not always reported, and because some of them combine sources in which class and source coincide, it remains open how much of their performance would carry over to new patients (\Ccontrib{1}, \Ccontrib{2}). A recent subject-disjoint benchmark on C-NMC found an AUROC of 0.913 \cite{albzour2026leakage}, which suggests that the split matters, but it covered a single, cell-level label.

\subsection{Patient-Level and Weakly Supervised Diagnosis}
Clinical decisions are made per patient, and several groups have moved in that direction with MIL. Expert-level single-cell classification \cite{matek2019blast,matek2021bmc} made it possible to identify genetic AML subtypes from blood-smear cells \cite{hehr2023scemila} and NPM1 or FLT3-ITD status from marrow \cite{wei2025annotationfree,eckardt2022npm1}. For APL, Sidhom \emph{et al.} \cite{sidhom2021apl} released the cohort used here and showed that a single-cell model trained on the earlier patients reached a cell-level AUC of 0.890 in development and 0.743 in the later validation cohort. Manescu \emph{et al.} \cite{manescu2023apl} trained an annotation-free MIL model on the same patients and reported an AUC of 0.96 under random three-fold CV. Interpretable morphometry has also proved useful: engineered cell features with sparse regression detected myelodysplasia and anemia subtypes and held up in an external cohort \cite{dealmeida2023haemorasis}. Across these studies, the number of missed patients at a clinically chosen operating point and the calibration of predicted probabilities are rarely reported (\Ccontrib{3}), and engineered features have not yet been compared with deep features for acute leukemia under the same protocol (\Ccontrib{4}).

\subsection{Foundation Models and Generalization}
Hematology foundation models aim to make cell representations robust to scanners and stains. DinoBloom, trained on more than 380,000 cells from 13 datasets, outperforms generic encoders \cite{koch2024dinobloom}; later work adapts DINOv3 with a risk-aware loss \cite{muminov2025cytodino} and compares foundation models with retrieval under a held-out-dataset protocol \cite{zamora2026retrieval}, while LeukemiaAttri provides images of the same films from different microscopes \cite{rehman2024leukemiaattri}. The largest patient-level study to date trained a DinoBloom-based transformer on 6,610 individuals and validated it on two external sets, including the cohort used here \cite{dasdelen2026caitomorph}. Such models need large labeled cohorts and GPU resources; whether lightweight models trained on a few hundred patients can be evaluated just as rigorously is the question behind \Ccontrib{5}.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset}
We used the public APL-AML cohort of Sidhom \emph{et al.} \cite{sidhom2021apl}. It contains single-cell images from Wright-stained peripheral blood smears of 106 adults with AML treated at Johns Hopkins Hospital between 2010 and 2020, captured with a CellaVision DM100 analyzer. Thirty-four patients have APL and 72 have other AML subtypes. The authors split the cohort by date: 82 patients diagnosed before January 2019 form the Discovery cohort and 24 diagnosed later form the Validation cohort. The 25,724 cell images measure about $360\times363$ pixels; 15,632 of them carry a cell type assigned by the analyzer and confirmed by its operator, and the remaining 10,092 are unlabeled. Table~\ref{tab:dataset} and Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset} describe the composition.

The cohort has several characteristics that shape the analysis. The classes are imbalanced and the imbalance shifts between cohorts: APL makes up 27\% of Discovery patients but 50\% of Validation patients, so accuracy alone would mean different things in the two cohorts. The number of cells per patient is confounded with the diagnosis in opposite directions---Discovery APL patients have about twice as many cells as AML patients, whereas the reverse holds in Validation---so our patient-level models are designed to be insensitive to bag size. The original authors note that all Discovery smears scanned before 2018 were APL \cite{sidhom2021apl}, which means that acquisition date carries diagnostic information inside the Discovery cohort and invites shortcut learning whenever patients leak across splits. The mix of cell types also differs by diagnosis; labeled monocytes, for example, are nearly absent in APL. Finally, ten image files are truncated, 54 are full microscope fields rather than single-cell crops, cell labels are analyzer classifications verified by an operator rather than consensus annotations, and the Validation cohort comes from the same hospital and contains only 24 patients.

To test whether our findings hold beyond one hospital, we repeated the analyses on a second public dataset, C-NMC 2019 \cite{gupta2022cnmc}, obtained from Kaggle. It contains 10,661 segmented single-cell images ($450\times450$ pixels, cell on a black background) from 73 subjects: 47 with B-lineage acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and 26 healthy individuals whose normal B cells serve as controls. The images were acquired in a different laboratory, with a different stain and microscope, from the APL-AML cohort. The challenge organizers distributed the training data in three subject-disjoint folds. Before any analysis we designated folds 0 and 1 as the development set (42 subjects, 7,108 cells; 30 with ALL) and fold 2 as the external test set (31 subjects, 3,553 cells; 17 with ALL). Every C-NMC cell is labeled with its subject's diagnosis, so the label belongs to the subject, as in T1 below; unlike the APL-AML cells, however, the ALL cells were selected as malignant lymphoblasts, so the label is also partly a property of the cell.

\subsection{Learning Tasks}
We defined five tasks (Table~\ref{tab:tasks}). In T1 every APL-AML cell inherits its patient's diagnosis, which reproduces a common way of building single-cell datasets. In T2 the label is the cell's own type (blast versus other labeled white cells, excluding artefacts, unidentified cells, smudge cells and platelets). T3 is the clinical question: does this patient have APL? T4 and T5 are the C-NMC counterparts of T1 and T3: classifying single cells, and classifying subjects, as ALL or normal.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Cell Segmentation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc} outlines the pipeline. A manifest records every image with its patient, cohort, diagnosis and cell label. Unreadable files are kept as missing rows and imputed inside each training fold, and no image is removed because of its label. The Validation cohort is never used for fitting, threshold selection or calibration.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{fig_preprocessing.pdf}
\caption{Preprocessing pipeline for both datasets. The orange steps prevent leakage: the external sets are fixed before any analysis, and every statistic---imputation, scaling, PCA, class weights and the decision threshold---is fitted inside the training fold.}
\label{fig:preproc}
\end{figure}

For the engineered features, each image is resized to $192\times192$ pixels and separated into hematoxylin, eosin and residual channels by color deconvolution \cite{ruifrok2001deconvolution}. The nucleus is segmented by Otsu thresholding \cite{otsu1979} of the smoothed hematoxylin channel, followed by morphological opening and selection of the component nearest the image center. We found that pale cytoplasm could not be separated reliably from neighboring red cells by color, so instead of claiming a full cell outline we describe the cytoplasm with two proxies: a basophilic region of blue-dominant pixels within one nuclear radius, and a perinuclear ring 2--9 pixels wide that excludes the bright background (Fig.~\ref{fig:seg}). C-NMC cells are already segmented and lie on a black background, which the stain-based nucleus segmentation would read as dense chromatin. For C-NMC we therefore set pixels whose brightest channel is below 15 to white before extracting engineered features, so that the same segmentation applies; the CNN received the original images.

\subsection{Feature Representations}
The 93 engineered features fall into six groups: nuclear shape (9 features: area, perimeter, circularity, eccentricity, solidity, extent, axis ratio, major axis and number of nuclear fragments); basophilic-region shape, nucleus-to-region ratio and rim size (10); stain and color statistics, i.e., hematoxylin and eosin optical density, CIE $L^*a^*b^*$ and saturation in the nucleus and the ring (26); chromatin texture from gray-level co-occurrence matrices \cite{haralick1973texture} at three distances together with nuclear entropy (16); uniform local binary patterns \cite{ojala2002lbp} at two radii (28); and counts and areas of pale nuclear spots and dark cytoplasmic granules (4). None of them depends on image size, file format or the source of the image.

As a deep representation we used a frozen EfficientNet-B0 \cite{tan2019efficientnet} pretrained on ImageNet, which maps each $224\times224$ input to a 1,280-dimensional vector. The fusion representation concatenates the engineered features and the embedding after column-wise standardization. Because a frozen generic encoder may understate what deep learning can do, we also fine-tuned EfficientNet-B0 on the APL-AML cells. Full fine-tuning was not feasible on our CPU-only workstation, so we froze the stem and the first five stages, stored their output once, and trained the last two stages, the 1,280-channel head and a new classifier, starting from the ImageNet weights. Training used AdamW with a cosine learning-rate schedule, early stopping on validation patients drawn from the training fold, and up to 5,000 training cells per fold (Table~\ref{tab:hparams}). The released code also supports the DinoBloom foundation model \cite{koch2024dinobloom}; it was not evaluated here because its weights could not be downloaded in our computing environment.

On top of these representations we tested feature reduction and selection steps, each fitted inside the training folds only. For dimensionality reduction, the cell embeddings $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{1280}$ of the training cells are standardized, $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\oslash\boldsymbol{\sigma}$, and projected onto the leading $k$ eigenvectors $\mathbf{W}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{1280\times k}$ of their covariance matrix \cite{jolliffe2016pca},
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{W}_k^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\qquad \rho_k=\frac{\sum_{j\le k}\lambda_j}{\sum_{j}\lambda_j},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_j$ are the eigenvalues and $\rho_k$ is the fraction of variance retained. We pre-specified $k\in\{16,32,64\}$ and added $k\in\{4,8,128,256\}$ as a sensitivity analysis. Because a patient vector holds five statistics per dimension, PCA reduces the patient-level input from 6,400 to $5k$ values. The pooled statistics of the component scores are centered and divided by one common scale, so that the components keep their relative variances. Standardizing each component separately instead would whiten the representation: the low-variance components, which carry mostly noise, would be magnified to the same size as the leading ones and weighted equally by the L2 penalty of the classifier. Our first analysis used per-component standardization; we report both variants (Section~\ref{sec:pca}). The other steps were a correlation filter that removes the later feature of every pair with $|r|>0.9$, per-slide standardization of the stain features, L1-penalized selection, and a ``digital differential'' that describes each patient by the predicted proportions of eight cell classes (Table~\ref{tab:hparams}).

\subsection{Classification Models}
Fig.~\ref{fig:model} shows how the components fit together. At cell level (T1, T2) we used L2-regularized logistic regression (LR) and LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, both with class-balanced weights. Each model is a single pipeline that imputes and scales features using training data only.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_model.pdf}
\caption{Model overview. Each cell is described by engineered features, a frozen CNN embedding (optionally reduced by PCA), their fusion, or a partially fine-tuned CNN with average or attention pooling. Cell-level classifiers serve T1, T2 and T4. For patients and subjects (T3, T5), the cells are combined by pooled statistics, by gated attention MIL or by averaging fine-tuned cell probabilities, and the three can be combined in a pre-specified ensemble. The decision threshold is set on development data for a recall of at least 0.95.}
\label{fig:model}
\end{figure*}

For patient diagnosis (T3) each patient is represented by up to 500 randomly sampled cells. The pooled baseline summarizes every feature by its mean, standard deviation and 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles and applies LR. The gated attention MIL model \cite{ilse2018abmil} maps each cell to $\mathbf{h}_k=\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}_k)$ with 128 units, weights the cells by
\begin{equation}
a_k=\frac{\exp\{\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\tanh(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{h}_k)\odot\sigma(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_k))\}}{\sum_{j}\exp\{\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\tanh(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{h}_j)\odot\sigma(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{h}_j))\}},
\end{equation}
and classifies the weighted average $\mathbf{z}=\sum_k a_k\mathbf{h}_k$. Because the weights sum to one, the model does not depend on how many cells a patient has. It is trained for 40 epochs with AdamW (learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$), class-weighted cross-entropy and random removal of 20\% of cells as augmentation.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Metrics}
Every model was evaluated in three ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:splits}): 5-fold CV on the Discovery cohort with image-level folds, as is common in the literature; 5-fold CV with patient-grouped folds, in which no patient appears in two folds; and a single external test on the Validation cohort. Within each training fold, a further 80/20 split of the same kind was used to fit a calibration temperature \cite{guo2017calibration}. For the external test of T3, the prediction for each Validation patient is the average of all fold models over three seeds, and the decision threshold is chosen on the out-of-fold Discovery predictions of the same models.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_splits.pdf}
\caption{Evaluation protocols. (a) Image-level splitting places cells of the same patient in training and test sets. (b) Patient-grouped splitting with an external cohort, used in this work; the external cohort is scored once.}
\label{fig:splits}
\end{figure*}

Since missing an APL patient can delay life-saving treatment, whereas a false alarm leads only to a confirmatory genetic test, we fixed every decision threshold in advance to reach a sensitivity of at least 0.95 on development data. At that operating point we report sensitivity (recall) together with the number of missed positives, specificity, F1 score, negative predictive value (NPV) and accuracy. AUROC is reported as a threshold-free summary, and calibration as the expected calibration error (ECE, 15 bins). Confidence intervals (95\%) come from bootstrap resampling of patients \cite{efron1986bootstrap}, because cells from the same patient are correlated, and differences between two models are tested with a paired patient-level bootstrap. When a table reports several comparisons against the same reference, $p$-values are also given after Holm correction \cite{holm1979}. C-NMC was analyzed with exactly the same protocol, with folds 0--1 in the role of Discovery and fold 2 in the role of Validation.

Three further analyses address questions that the external tests alone cannot answer. First, because 24 external patients give wide confidence intervals, we repeated the main patient-level comparisons with three repeats of 5-fold patient-level CV over all 106 APL-AML patients, stratified by diagnosis and cohort; thresholds were chosen by an inner CV inside each training fold. This analysis has more statistical power but gives up the temporal separation, so it complements rather than replaces the external test. Second, to see whether a new laboratory could restore the intended operating point, we simulated local recalibration: $k$ confirmed local patients ($k/2$ APL, $k/2$ AML, $k=2,\dots,8$) were drawn from the Validation cohort, the threshold was lowered if needed to just below the lowest score of the local APL cases, and the remaining $24-k$ patients were scored, over 2,000 random draws. Third, decision-curve analysis \cite{vickers2006dca} estimated the net benefit of acting on the model's output at an APL prevalence of 10\%, closer to that among patients with suspected acute leukemia than the 50\% of the Validation cohort; predicted probabilities were mapped to risks by logistic recalibration on Discovery and shifted to the target prevalence.

All experiments ran on a CPU-only workstation without a GPU (Table~\ref{tab:env}). \subsection{Effect of the Evaluation Protocol}
Table~\ref{tab:leakage} and Fig.~\ref{fig:leakage} compare the three protocols. When each cell carried its patient's diagnosis (T1), image-level CV reported AUROCs of 0.938--0.987. Grouping folds by patient lowered them to 0.867--0.901, and the external cohort to 0.658--0.734, so image-level CV overstated external performance by 0.25 to 0.32. When the label described the cell itself (T2), the three protocols nearly agreed and the overstatement was 0.013--0.036. The models themselves changed little: a classifier trained under the leaky protocol scored about the same on the external cohort as one trained under the grouped protocol. What changed was the number reported. The same pattern appeared on C-NMC (Table~\ref{tab:cnmc}a), where every cell also carries its subject's diagnosis: image-level CV reported 0.912--0.939, subject-grouped CV 0.834--0.864 and the external fold 0.775--0.840, an overstatement of 0.09--0.14. The inflation was smaller than for T1, consistent with C-NMC labels being partly a property of the cell, since the ALL cells were selected as malignant lymphoblasts.

For blast detection on new patients, engineered features with LightGBM reached an AUROC of 0.957 (95\% CI 0.931--0.977) and detected 99\% of blasts at a specificity of 0.62, whereas EfficientNet-B0 embeddings reached 0.920. \subsection{Feature Engineering}
Table~\ref{tab:fe} reports the feature-engineering experiments for patient-level APL detection. On the Validation cohort, dimensionality reduction had the largest effect. The configuration chosen by the rule fixed in advance---the highest Discovery CV AUROC---was PCA with 64 components, which raised external AUROC from 0.743 to 0.819; 32 components gave the highest external value (0.833), and 16 components the best operating point, detecting 11 of 12 APL patients at a specificity of 0.67. None of these gains was statistically significant, and after Holm correction no feature-engineering step differed significantly from its unreduced reference. Section~\ref{sec:pca} shows that the PCA gain did not reproduce in larger or independent data. Removing redundant engineered features changed little. Normalizing stain features within each slide and L1 selection both lowered external AUROC, to 0.58 and 0.59: the color of a patient's cells carries diagnostic information---APL promyelocytes are heavily granulated---that slide-wise normalization removes, and L1 selection with 82 patients chose unstable features. A ``digital differential'' built from the predicted proportions of eight cell classes reached 0.785 and detected all 12 APL patients, at a specificity of 0.33.

\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction: Before and After PCA}
\label{sec:pca}
Table~\ref{tab:pcaimpact} summarizes the impact of PCA on the pooled patient model built from EfficientNet-B0 embeddings, and Table~\ref{tab:pca} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pca} give the full sweep over the number of components on both datasets. Without reduction, each patient is described by 6,400 values (five statistics of 1,280 dimensions), about 100 for every APL-AML training patient.

\emph{Before and after on APL-AML.} PCA improved how well patients were ranked but not the decisions made at the clinical operating point. External AUROC rose from 0.708 without PCA to 0.785 with 32 and 0.792 with 64 components, and stayed at 0.778 up to 256 components; the pre-declared rule (highest Discovery CV) selected 256 components. At the threshold fixed on Discovery, however, the unreduced model detected 8 of 12 APL patients and the reduced models 6 of 12, at specificities of 0.67 and 0.75; accuracy fell from 0.67 to 0.63 and F1 from 0.67 to 0.57. Fewer than 16 components discarded diagnostic information (8 components: AUROC 0.646).

\emph{Before and after on C-NMC.} Here PCA cost a little on every metric. External AUROC was 0.908 without PCA and rose steadily with the number of components (8: 0.803; 32: 0.840; 64: 0.853; 256: 0.870) without reaching the unreduced value, and the pre-declared rule selected no reduction. At the operating point, the unreduced model detected all 17 ALL subjects with an accuracy of 0.84 and F1 of 0.87; with 32--64 components, 16 of 17 were detected, accuracy was 0.71--0.74 and F1 0.78--0.80.

\emph{With more statistical power.} In pooled CV over all 106 APL-AML patients, 32 and 64 components ranked patients as well as the unreduced embedding ($\Delta$AUROC $+0.000$ and $+0.006$; Table~\ref{tab:pooled}), and 16 components were significantly worse ($-0.057$). The APL-AML gain on the 24-patient temporal test therefore did not reflect a general advantage.

\emph{A pitfall: whitening.} In our first analysis the pooled component scores were standardized one by one before the classifier, as is common practice. This whitens the representation: low-variance components, which mostly carry noise, are inflated to the same scale as the leading ones and weighted equally by the L2 penalty. With whitening, external AUROC behaved erratically---on C-NMC it fell from 0.849 at 8 to 0.655 at 256 components, far below the unreduced 0.908, while development CV rose to 0.99 (Table~\ref{tab:pca}, column Std.; dashed lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:pca})---and on APL-AML it produced the largest apparent gain (0.833 with 32 components). Keeping the components at their natural scale removed both effects. The rising development CV together with falling external AUROC is the signature of a classifier fitting noise directions, and it is a warning that dimensionality and scaling choices should not be tuned on development CV.

\emph{Why PCA gives no consistent edge.} PCA keeps the directions of largest variance, not those that separate the classes. The diagnostics in Fig.~\ref{fig:pca}c show that the external cell-level disease signal increased steadily with the number of components on both datasets, so the discarded low-variance components still carry diagnostic information---especially on C-NMC, where malignant and normal B cells differ subtly. Nor did PCA remove acquisition-specific information: a classifier separating external from development cells of the control class performed about equally on every representation (APL-AML 0.57--0.64; C-NMC 0.45--0.56). What PCA does is shrink the patient-level model, and on the shifted APL-AML cohort this improved ranking, presumably by discarding some development-specific variation together with some signal; on C-NMC, without such a shift, the lost signal dominated. Unsupervised dimensionality reduction is therefore a reasonable way to make patient-level models smaller, but not a reliable way to make them more accurate.

\subsection{Training Dynamics and Learning Curves}
\label{sec:training}
Fig.~\ref{fig:training} shows how the trainable models learned (PCA scores standardized per component, as in our first analysis). These curves were computed on the Discovery folds after the configuration had been fixed and were not used to choose epochs, rounds or thresholds. The attention MIL model was trained with a constant learning rate of $2\times10^{-4}$ for 40 epochs. On the full 1,280-dimensional embedding, its training loss fell almost to zero within 10 epochs (0.024). The loss on the held-out fold was lowest at epoch 4 (0.28) and then doubled to 0.60 by epoch 40, while held-out AUROC stayed at 0.96--0.97 (Fig.~\ref{fig:training}a--c). The model kept ranking patients well but grew increasingly overconfident, in line with its poor external calibration (ECE 0.36, Table~\ref{tab:patient}). With PCA-32 inputs, learning was slower and steadier: held-out loss decreased until epoch 31 (0.33) and ended at 0.38, the lowest final value of the three inputs. Engineered features were in between, with a minimum at epoch 10 (0.38) and 0.52 at epoch 40. For all three inputs, held-out AUROC plateaued after about 10 epochs, so the fixed schedule did not cost ranking performance. For the full embedding, however, a shorter schedule would have given better-calibrated probabilities.

LightGBM at cell level (T1, Fig.~\ref{fig:training}d) behaved similarly. With a learning rate of 0.05, held-out log-loss was lowest after 95 boosting rounds (0.363) and rose to 0.426 at 400 rounds, while training loss kept falling to 0.034.

The learning curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:training}e--f show what limits the patient-level models. We trained the pooled model on 16 Discovery patients per fold and up to all of them (65--66). Every representation separated its training patients perfectly at every size (training AUROC 1.00), and held-out-fold AUROC rose to 0.93--0.97 for all three. On the external cohort, however, the curves diverged. PCA-32 was ahead of the full embedding at every training-set size: 0.59 vs.\ 0.49 with 16 patients, 0.73 vs.\ 0.64 with 24, 0.80 vs.\ 0.72 with 48 and 0.82 vs.\ 0.72 with all patients. With only 24 patients, PCA-32 matched the full embedding trained on all of them, whose curve had flattened after 48 patients, whereas the PCA-32 curve was still rising. On this temporal test, reducing the dimension bought the equivalent of more than twice as many training patients; Section~\ref{sec:pca} shows, however, that this advantage did not carry over to pooled CV or to C-NMC.

\subsection{Patient-Level Diagnosis and the Operating Point}
Table~\ref{tab:patient} lists the patient-level models without feature engineering. Attention MIL on engineered features ranked patients best (external AUROC 0.812, 95\% CI 0.614--0.963). The operating point, however, did not transfer. A threshold that detected at least 95\% of APL patients in Discovery labeled every Validation patient as APL for this model, and with EfficientNet-B0 features the same rule detected only 6 of 12 APL patients (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}). Youden thresholds traded the other way, missing half of the APL patients. Even a threshold re-set on the Validation cohort itself, which is not possible in practice, could have cleared at most about half of the AML patients without missing an APL patient (oracle specificity 0.42--0.50, Table~\ref{tab:fe}). The fine-tuned CNN, with cell probabilities averaged per patient as in \cite{sidhom2021apl}, reached an external AUROC of 0.778 (0.576--0.938) and detected 10 of 12 APL patients at a specificity of 0.67, so the cell-level gain from fine-tuning did not translate into better patient-level diagnosis.

Table~\ref{tab:cnmc}b repeats the patient-level experiment on C-NMC subjects (T5). \subsection{Analysis With More Statistical Power}
\label{sec:pooled}
Table~\ref{tab:pooled} shows the pooled CV over all 106 APL-AML patients. With 34 APL patients in the test folds, several differences became significant. The unreduced EfficientNet-B0 embedding ranked patients best (AUROC 0.953, 95\% CI 0.898--0.989), better than engineered features with pooled statistics ($-0.059$, Holm-adjusted $p<0.05$) and better than the promyelocyte fraction used as a manual biomarker (0.677). PCA with 32 or 64 components did not change the ranking, whether the component scores were whitened ($-0.005$ and $-0.011$) or kept at their natural scale ($+0.000$ and $+0.006$), and 16 components lost signal ($-0.074$ and $-0.057$). Thresholds chosen inside each training fold transferred to the held-out folds (recall 0.89--1.00), which supports the interpretation that the failure on the Validation cohort reflects temporal shift. Because patients from both periods were mixed, this analysis does not measure transfer to a later cohort and should be read alongside, not instead of, the external results.

\subsection{Can a New Laboratory Restore the Operating Point?}
\label{sec:recal}
Table~\ref{tab:recal} and Fig.~\ref{fig:recal} simulate a laboratory that confirms $k$ local patients before deploying a model. Setting the threshold just below the lowest score of the $m=k/2$ local APL cases gave the same expected recall for every model, about $m/(m+1)$: 0.50 with one local APL case and 0.80 with four (Fig.~\ref{fig:recal}a). This follows from the exchangeability of the local and future APL scores and does not depend on how well the model ranks patients, so about 19 confirmed local APL cases are needed before such a threshold can be expected to reach a recall of 0.95. Combining the development threshold with the local cases---lowering it only when a local APL case would otherwise be missed---raised recall from 0.50 to 0.80 with eight local cases for the frozen CNN models whose development threshold had missed half of the APL patients, at the cost of specificity (from 0.67--0.92 down to 0.44--0.49; Fig.~\ref{fig:recal}b). Even then, the probability of missing at least one APL patient among the remaining 16 was 0.66--0.68. Decision curves at an APL prevalence of 10\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:recal}c) showed net benefits close to those of testing every patient at threshold probabilities up to 5\%, and a small positive benefit above that; at a 5\% threshold, the attention MIL and fine-tuned models gave net benefits of 0.072 and 0.059 against 0.053 for testing everyone.

\subsection{Comparison With Existing Methods on the Same Cohort}
Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares our models with existing methods on the same patients. The original study reported a cell-level AUC of 0.743 in the later cohort \cite{sidhom2021apl}, and Manescu \emph{et al.} reported 0.96 with random three-fold CV \cite{manescu2023apl}; neither reported results at a fixed sensitivity. Because the protocols differ, we re-ran representative designs on the identical split with the same operating-point rule. The promyelocyte proportion, the biomarker used as comparator in \cite{sidhom2021apl}, reached an AUROC of 0.611, and a model on the full differential count reached 0.681. Averaging single-cell predictions per patient, as in \cite{sidhom2021apl}, reached 0.681--0.799 depending on the features, and the CNN-feature-plus-SVM design of \cite{kasim2025multiclass} reached 0.806. Our best patient-level models reached 0.812 without feature engineering and 0.833 with PCA. With 24 external patients the confidence intervals of all learned models overlap; the clearest gain is over the manual biomarkers, and the clearest difference between protocols is the gap between random CV (0.96) and temporal validation.

\subsection{Ablation and Interpretation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:impact} show which engineered feature groups matter for blast detection. Removing the stain and color group cost $-0.034$ AUROC (95\% CI $-0.060$ to $-0.015$), chromatin texture $-0.012$ and the basophilic region $-0.005$; nuclear shape, local binary patterns and the nucleolus and granule proxies were redundant given the other groups. \section{Discussion}
Four findings stand out. First, the amount of leakage depends on what the label describes, and this held on both datasets. When a label belongs to the patient, every cell carries the same stain, smear and scan date, and a model can learn these instead of the disease. In APL-AML, where scan date and diagnosis are partly confounded \cite{sidhom2021apl}, the inflation reached 0.32 AUROC. In C-NMC, where the label belongs to the subject but the malignant cells were also selected as such, it was 0.09--0.14. When the label described the cell itself it was at most 0.04. Image-level results for diagnosis-level labels should therefore be read with caution. This may also explain why studies that held out whole subjects, such as the C-NMC challenge entries \cite{prellberg2019cnmc,mondal2021ensemble}, report F1 scores near 0.89 rather than near 1.0.

Second, ranking transferred better than decisions did, and whether decisions transferred depended on the kind of shift. On a random subject-disjoint split of C-NMC, the threshold fixed for a sensitivity of 0.95 caught 15--17 of 17 ALL subjects; on the later APL-AML cohort, the same rule flagged everyone or missed half of the APL patients, even though the models ranked patients reasonably well. Stain and color, which carry real diagnostic information, drift between the APL-AML cohorts (Fig.~\ref{fig:eda}), and pooled CV without that drift gave thresholds that transferred. Local recalibration is the obvious remedy, but our simulation sets a sobering bound: a threshold placed below the lowest of $m$ local positive cases has an expected recall of only $m/(m+1)$, whatever the model, so about 20 confirmed local cases are needed before a recall of 0.95 can be expected. For a condition in which a missed diagnosis can be fatal, a model like this is best used to prioritize smears for urgent review rather than to rule APL out.

Third, no single representation won everywhere. Engineered features, which are interpretable, were best for blast detection and, alone or fused with CNN features, on both C-NMC tasks; fine-tuning a CNN gave the best single-cell APL classifier on the external cohort; and the unreduced CNN embedding ranked patients best in pooled CV. Removing stain information consistently hurt, because in APL the color of the cytoplasm is part of the disease. Attention MIL ranked patients well but was not significantly better than averaging cell predictions, in line with the modest gains reported for MIL on small cohorts.

Fourth, PCA did not give a consistent edge, and an implementation detail decided how it looked. With the component scores standardized one by one---a common default---PCA appeared to be the largest improvement on APL-AML and a severe loss on C-NMC, while development CV kept rising. Kept at their natural scale, the components behaved as expected: performance approached that of the full embedding as components were added, PCA improved ranking but not decisions on the shifted APL-AML cohort, was neutral in pooled CV, and cost a few points on C-NMC. PCA keeps variance, not diagnostic signal, and the low-variance components still carried disease information. We report these results in full because a single small external test, combined with an untested preprocessing default, can make a chance improvement look like a method.

This study has limitations. The two datasets differ in disease (APL vs.\ other AML; ALL vs.\ normal B cells), so the replication tests the evaluation principles rather than one clinical model across sites; an independent AML cohort with APL cases remains to be tested. The APL-AML external cohort contains 24 patients and the C-NMC external fold 31 subjects, so patient-level confidence intervals are wide. Cell labels in APL-AML come from an analyzer verified by an operator rather than from expert consensus. Our cytoplasm features rely on proxies, and segmentation fails on some small cells. Fine-tuning was partial (the last two stages), limited to 5,000 cells per fold and run with one seed on a CPU, so a fully fine-tuned or larger network could do better. Hematology foundation models such as DinoBloom \cite{koch2024dinobloom} were implemented but could not be evaluated in our computing environment.

\section{Conclusion}
Reported accuracy for leukemia detection from blood smears depends strongly on how the evaluation is set up. Across two public datasets, splitting cells instead of patients inflated AUROC by up to 0.32 when cells carried the patient's diagnosis. Under patient-level evaluation with external test sets, interpretable engineered features matched or exceeded generic CNN features in most comparisons, fine-tuning helped single-cell classification but not patient diagnosis, and PCA, once its components were kept at their natural scale, gave no consistent improvement. Decision thresholds transferred to a random held-out split but not to a later cohort, and restoring them locally requires about 20 confirmed positive cases. We recommend that studies in this area split data by patient, state whether labels describe cells or patients, test on more than one external set, report sensitivity, missed cases, specificity, F1 score, NPV and calibration at a pre-specified operating point, and re-check apparent improvements with more data before adopting them.
